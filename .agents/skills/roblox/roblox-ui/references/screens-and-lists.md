# Screens, lists and windows

Contents: **Screen composition and controls**, **Lists with a detail side**, **States and legibility**, **Notices**, **Artwork and label space**, **Shared windows**.

## Screen composition and controls

Give each screen one clear purpose and a recognizable primary action. Use plain player-facing labels, readable type, consistent spacing, alignment, color, and shared controls. Controls side by side, such as a text box and the buttons that act on it, keep the theme's standard gap between them, never touching or a few pixels apart. Text of one role, such as a list row's name, a description or a field label, uses one size in every panel of the game, never smaller in one panel than in another. Keep related actions together; avoid crowded panels and redundant instructions. Every button does something the player chooses to do now: a state such as saved, done or equipped is shown as text or a mark, never drawn as a button, and a change the player makes, such as arranging a loadout or picking an option, takes effect and saves as it is made, never waiting on a Save button. Make the next step understandable to someone seeing the game for the first time. Explain unfamiliar actions when needed without exposing internal IDs or implementation details. Tabs or categories that divide a list name the kinds of thing it holds, such as helmets and boots in an equipment inventory; an attribute some entries share, such as being rare, newly found or seasonal, is a filter over those kinds, never a category of its own, so no entry is split from its own kind. A list holding many entries of several kinds, such as a shop, an inventory or an admin tool's item list, is divided into those kinds beside its search, so nobody has to know a name or scroll the whole list to find one; where a list has no detail side, an entry's details show on hover, and on press-and-hold on touch. A list is divided into sub-categories only when it holds more than one kind the player picks between; a lone group, or one that only renames its category, such as a lone "Hand tools" group as the only group under Tools, is left out. A list or inventory shows its entries and their own details, never a line about where entries come from, such as the shop, place or character that sells or gives them or where they are found or caught, which goes stale as the game adds sources; a list with no entries shows one short line saying the player has none of that kind. Every tab in one row acts on the same subject, such as the vehicle a garage window shows: a control that picks that subject sits beside the tabs, never as a tab of its own, and an action on a different subject, such as one that moves the player rather than the vehicle, is labeled with that subject.

Give every action control its name, or a one-line effect, beside its icon; an icon or a key letter alone leaves players guessing what an ability does. Show the input that triggers each action for the device in use (the key on keyboard, the button on gamepad, the on-screen control on touch), and update it when the player switches device.

Review the whole screen before individual controls. Identify the focal point and primary action; inspect the composition’s position, proportions, whitespace, and balance against the usable viewport. A centered title does not center an off-center group. A 3D preview, such as a character, vehicle or creature in a viewport, centers its subject on the subject's rendered bounds, recomputed whenever the shown item changes the silhouette, and frames it so the part the preview exists to show, such as a vehicle's new wheels or a pet's saddle, is visible, faces the viewer and fills enough of the frame to judge; a character preview has its rig and accessories fitted as `roblox-characters` says before the camera is fit, and stands at rest or in its idle animation, never frozen in the pose the live character held when the window opened, such as mid-run. For selection screens, bound and align the title, full previews, confirmation, and status as one composition; preserve intentional asymmetry when the design calls for it. Repeated items shown side by side, such as cards in a row, list rows or grid tiles, share one layout: each shared element, such as a name, a bar, an icon or a price, sits at the same position in every item, so it lines up across the set. Content only some items carry, such as a status line or a badge, takes reserved space or goes after the shared elements, never centering that moves them, and the items share one size. Inspect the set with the optional content on some items and off others. A region left mostly empty while its content could use the space, such as a sidebar holding one small control or a list that stops a third of the way down its column with more entries hidden below, is a layout defect: give that space to content the screen already shows, such as letting the list fill its column. A fixed-size window holding few entries is not this defect, since its size stays for when it holds more. Check safe areas and the surrounding world, HUD, notices, and overlays together on wide, narrow, and short screens. Record the visual defect and repair even when every control is clickable and inside its bounds.

## Lists with a detail side

A list with a detail or preview side, such as a store, an inventory, a loadout or a level picker, shows the selected entry there, and the player selects by clicking, tapping or pressing. Hover changes only the hovered control's own look, such as its highlight or outline, never the detail side's text, art or preview, so sweeping the pointer across the list moves nothing else and a touch player, who cannot hover, sees what a mouse player sees. The detail side shows the selected entry whole: its name, art and stats, and anything it grants or contains, such as an ability, a companion or a recipe, shown as the game shows that thing elsewhere, on the same card with its own art, stats and what it does. A sentence such as "Includes Frost Bolt" names the thing without telling the player what it does or whether it is worth buying.

## States and legibility

Make selected, hovered, focused, disabled, pending, successful, and failed states distinct. A control shows only when its action applies to the player's current state: one with nothing to act on, such as leaving a group the player is not in or removing an item they have not equipped, is left out, never shown disabled. Show a control disabled only when its action applies but cannot happen yet, such as a purchase the player cannot afford, and explain why where useful. Do not rely on color alone. Keep text legible over the world: give it its own contrast with a heavy weight and dark stroke or an opaque panel behind it, and check it against the brightest and busiest backgrounds it can appear over, since thin, gray or small text on a translucent panel loses contrast over bright scenes. Accommodate wrapping and long content, keep transitions short and purposeful, and avoid tiny targets or decorative motion that delays actions. Respect reduced motion and preserve keyboard/gamepad focus where supported.

## Notices

Text the player must notice goes where their eyes already are: an instruction they must read to continue, and the outcome of a deliberate action they chose and are waiting on, such as finishing a craft, claiming a reward or a refused attempt; frequent routine feedback, such as a small gain from each pickup, is not this card. Put it in a framed card near the center of the screen, or beside the thing it asks them to act on or the thing their action changed, in type larger than the surrounding HUD or panel text, never a line of small text tucked into a corner, a footer or a toolbar. An outcome is transient: it holds long enough to read, about two to three seconds, then fades without blocking the next action, while a refusal's next step stays until the player acts. Inspect every transient notice, this card included, together with the persistent HUD: a notice can cover a persistent objective or the primary action even when each component fits on its own, so reserve space or place it where it covers no active control, and verify short and wrapped messages on desktop and phone. Judge the card as a first-time player would meet it, from a full-screen capture at each supported size: someone who has not looked for it must notice it within a second.

## Artwork and label space

Give artwork, names, costs, and status labels dedicated layout space. A description shown beside an item's stats is flavor in the game's own voice, saying what the item is or feels like in its world, and never restates a number or effect the stats already show, such as a lantern that "lights any room" beside its listed light radius. A reward or level-up card shows each gain as its change, such as "+2 bag slots", with no running total beside it and no line restating an effect the player just saw happen, such as a bridge appearing. Do not place status text over artwork or squeeze long labels into tiny badges. A region whose content varies, between entries, as the selection changes or as a state comes and goes, reserves the space its largest content needs, such as two lines for a description that can wrap, so nothing beside or under it moves when the content changes: a preview or character that jumps as text reflows from one line to two is that defect. Check it by switching quickly between the shortest, longest and empty content. Check the longest real label at the smallest supported component size and in every relevant state; inspect rendered text, not only its container bounds.

## Shared windows

Share the theme, controls, modal shell, and preview adapters where the game uses them. Related windows use consistent close behavior, backdrop, focus/input handling, and dimensions. Every dismissible panel shows its close control in the same place across the game, by convention a high-contrast X at the top-right corner, never covered by other HUD elements, and it closes its panel even while other panels are open. A panel's title row holds only its title and close control at every screen size; its actions sit in their own row under the body, never directly under a tab row where they read as more tabs, compacted on short screens rather than moved beside the title. Reserve a panel whose only exit is a confirm button for a real decision. A confirmation before a destructive or hard-to-undo action is a modal dialog in the shared modal shell, the question with its cancel and action buttons, never an inline row swapped into the panel. A window's own subject, such as the player or pet it shows, has its details in the side that subject owns, never pushed into a tab's body. A second window replaces the first; repeated triggers follow the game's documented toggle behavior. A window the player carries, such as a journal, map or inventory, leaves movement and the camera working while it is open and takes input only on its panel; a window that belongs to standing somewhere, such as a workbench, board or travel gate, and exclusive start screens and modals block underlying pointer, keyboard, hover, and movement input, then restore it on exit.
