# Roblox UI

## Dependencies

- `luau`
- `roblox-worldbuilding`
- `roblox-characters`

## Clarity and visual quality

Follow `luau`'s required UI framework. Make every interface clean, easy to use, beginner friendly, and visually pleasing. Treat these as acceptance criteria, not optional polish after code compiles. Preserve the game's established artwork and visual identity unless a redesign is requested.

Apply `luau` whenever reading or editing its Luau code. Return required engine setup and playtest work to the caller. Read [Framework and component practices](references/components.md) before changing architecture, dependencies, lifecycle, or layout.

Give each screen one clear purpose and a recognizable primary action. Use plain player-facing labels, readable type, consistent spacing, alignment, color, and shared controls. Controls side by side, such as a text box and the buttons that act on it, keep the theme's standard gap between them, never touching or a few pixels apart. Text of one role, such as a list row's name, a description or a field label, uses one size in every panel of the game, never smaller in one panel than in another. Keep related actions together; avoid crowded panels and redundant instructions. Every button does something the player chooses to do now: a state such as saved, done or equipped is shown as text or a mark, never drawn as a button, and a change the player makes, such as arranging a loadout or picking an option, takes effect and saves as it is made, never waiting on a Save button. Make the next step understandable to someone seeing the game for the first time. Explain unfamiliar actions when needed without exposing internal IDs or implementation details. Tabs or categories that divide a list name the kinds of thing it holds, such as fish and insects in a collection log; an attribute some entries share, such as being rare, newly found or seasonal, is a filter over those kinds, never a category of its own, so no entry is split from its own kind. A list holding many entries of several kinds, such as a shop, an inventory or an admin tool's item list, is divided into those kinds beside its search, so nobody has to know a name or scroll the whole list to find one; where a list has no detail side, an entry's details show on hover, and on press-and-hold on touch. A list is divided into sub-categories only when it holds more than one kind the player picks between; a lone group, or one that only renames its category, such as a lone "Garden seeds" group as the only group under Seeds, is left out. A list or inventory shows its entries and their own details, never a line about where entries come from, such as the shop, place or character that sells or gives them, which goes stale as the game adds sources; a list with no entries shows one short line saying the player has none of that kind. Every tab in one row acts on the same subject, such as the pet a care window shows: a control that picks that subject sits beside the tabs, never as a tab of its own, and an action on a different subject, such as one that moves the player rather than the pet, is labeled with that subject.

Give every action control its name, or a one-line effect, beside its icon; an icon or a key letter alone leaves players guessing what an ability does. Show the input that triggers each action for the device in use (the key on keyboard, the button on gamepad, the on-screen control on touch), and update it when the player switches device.

Review the whole screen before individual controls. Identify the focal point and primary action; inspect the composition’s position, proportions, whitespace, and balance against the usable viewport. A centered title does not center an off-center group. A 3D preview, such as a character, vehicle or creature in a viewport, centers its subject on the subject's rendered bounds, recomputed whenever the shown item changes the silhouette, and frames it so the part the preview exists to show, such as a vehicle's new wheels or a pet's saddle, is visible, faces the viewer and fills enough of the frame to judge; a character preview has its rig and accessories fitted as `roblox-characters` says before the camera is fit, and stands at rest or in its idle animation, never frozen in the pose the live character held when the window opened, such as mid-run. For selection screens, bound and align the title, full previews, confirmation, and status as one composition; preserve intentional asymmetry when the design calls for it. Repeated items shown side by side, such as cards in a row, list rows or grid tiles, share one layout: each shared element, such as a name, a bar, an icon or a price, sits at the same position in every item, so it lines up across the set. Content only some items carry, such as a status line or a badge, takes reserved space or goes after the shared elements, never centering that moves them, and the items share one size. Inspect the set with the optional content on some items and off others. A region left mostly empty while its content could use the space, such as a sidebar holding one small control or a list that stops a third of the way down its column with more entries hidden below, is a layout defect: give that space to content the screen already shows, such as letting the list fill its column. A fixed-size window holding few entries is not this defect, since its size stays for when it holds more. Check safe areas and the surrounding world, HUD, notices, and overlays together on wide, narrow, and short screens. Record the visual defect and repair even when every control is clickable and inside its bounds.

A list with a detail or preview side, such as a store, an inventory, a loadout or a level picker, shows the selected entry there, and the player selects by clicking, tapping or pressing. Hover changes only the hovered control's own look, such as its highlight or outline, never the detail side's text, art or preview, so sweeping the pointer across the list moves nothing else and a touch player, who cannot hover, sees what a mouse player sees. The detail side shows the selected entry whole: its name, art and stats, and anything it grants or contains, such as an ability, a companion or a recipe, shown as the game shows that thing elsewhere, on the same card with its own art, stats and what it does. A sentence such as "Includes Turbo Dash" names the thing without telling the player what it does or whether it is worth buying.

Make selected, hovered, focused, disabled, pending, successful, and failed states distinct. A control shows only when its action applies to the player's current state: one with nothing to act on, such as leaving a group the player is not in or removing an item they have not equipped, is left out, never shown disabled. Show a control disabled only when its action applies but cannot happen yet, such as a purchase the player cannot afford, and explain why where useful. Do not rely on color alone. Keep text legible over the world: give it its own contrast with a heavy weight and dark stroke or an opaque panel behind it, and check it against the brightest and busiest backgrounds it can appear over, since thin, gray or small text on a translucent panel loses contrast over bright scenes. Accommodate wrapping and long content, keep transitions short and purposeful, and avoid tiny targets or decorative motion that delays actions. Respect reduced motion and preserve keyboard/gamepad focus where supported.

Text the player must notice goes where their eyes already are: an instruction they must read to continue, and the outcome of a deliberate action they chose and are waiting on, such as finishing a craft, claiming a reward or a refused attempt; frequent routine feedback, such as a small gain from each pickup, is not this card. Put it in a framed card near the center of the screen, or beside the thing it asks them to act on or the thing their action changed, in type larger than the surrounding HUD or panel text, never a line of small text tucked into a corner, a footer or a toolbar. An outcome is transient: it holds long enough to read, about two to three seconds, then fades without blocking the next action, while a refusal's next step stays until the player acts. Inspect every transient notice, this card included, together with the persistent HUD: a notice can cover a persistent objective or the primary action even when each component fits on its own, so reserve space or place it where it covers no active control, and verify short and wrapped messages on desktop and phone. Judge the card as a first-time player would meet it, from a full-screen capture at each supported size: someone who has not looked for it must notice it within a second.

Give artwork, names, costs, and status labels dedicated layout space. A description shown beside an item's stats is flavor in the game's own voice, saying what the item is or feels like in its world, and never restates a number or effect the stats already show, such as a fishing rod that "catches anything" beside its 100% catch rate. A reward or level-up card shows each gain as its change, such as "+2 bag slots", with no running total beside it and no line restating an effect the player just saw happen, such as a garden blooming. Do not place status text over artwork or squeeze long labels into tiny badges. A region whose content varies, between entries, as the selection changes or as a state comes and goes, reserves the space its largest content needs, such as two lines for a description that can wrap, so nothing beside or under it moves when the content changes: a preview or character that jumps as text reflows from one line to two is that defect. Check it by switching quickly between the shortest, longest and empty content. Check the longest real label at the smallest supported component size and in every relevant state; inspect rendered text, not only its container bounds.

For UI-owned world-space messages, apply `roblox-worldbuilding`'s world-space text inspection
alongside the component lifecycle and interaction checks below.

Share the theme, controls, modal shell, and preview adapters where the game uses them. Related windows use consistent close behavior, backdrop, focus/input handling, and dimensions. Every dismissible panel shows its close control in the same place across the game, by convention a high-contrast X at the top-right corner, never covered by other HUD elements, and it closes its panel even while other panels are open. A panel's title row holds only its title and close control at every screen size; its actions sit in their own row under the body, never directly under a tab row where they read as more tabs, compacted on short screens rather than moved beside the title. Reserve a panel whose only exit is a confirm button for a real decision. A confirmation before a destructive or hard-to-undo action is a modal dialog in the shared modal shell, the question with its cancel and action buttons, never an inline row swapped into the panel. A window's own subject, such as the player or pet it shows, has its details in the side that subject owns, never pushed into a tab's body. A second window replaces the first; repeated triggers follow the game's documented toggle behavior. A window the player carries, such as a journal, map or inventory, leaves movement and the camera working while it is open and takes input only on its panel; a window that belongs to standing somewhere, such as a workbench, board or travel gate, and exclusive start screens and modals block underlying pointer, keyboard, hover, and movement input, then restore it on exit.

Use contextual prompts for nearby interactions and actionable touch equivalents. A tutorial teaches the actual current controls one step at a time, with feedback and pointers attached to measured controls. The pointer already shows where to act, so each step's text is one short line saying what the action does for the player, such as "Tap Boost to pull ahead!" or "Place a plank to cross the gap.", never the click route the pointer shows, such as "Open your bag, choose the plank, then tap the gap to place it.", and never the tutorial's own bookkeeping, such as "Select Place to complete this step." A lesson shows no lesson title, step number or count of steps left, since none of them changes what the player does next. A lesson that explains before it asks for an action gives each page one idea, and its button reads "Next" on every page before the last and "Begin" on the last or only page, never "I understand", "OK" or "Got it", which ask the player to vouch for having read it. Every step whose target is away from the player draws the guide line from the player to it as well as its pointer, a step that is travel, such as riding a lift to the next floor, or a wait, such as an oven timer, as much as any other. While a step is active, hide a prompt that would send the player somewhere else, such as a conversation prompt left on a character whose part is done, or have the step's guide line point to the real target, since a player who sees that prompt may think they must use it again. If using animated pointers, give them a clear shape and a still reduced-motion state. Reserve a gutter beside the measured target; the entire animation envelope must stay outside instruction text, labels, and other controls. Positioning an arrow above a button does not establish that the space above it is empty. Dismiss blocking lessons before requiring the underlying action. Do not overwhelm beginners with controls or explanations that are not yet relevant.

## Responsive components

When a game has a persistent objective HUD, prioritize the next useful action over long headings and paragraphs. Keep a slim reminder visible: the goal with a live count toward its target, such as "Repair lanterns 2/3", plus its reward when it has one; during a guided sequence it holds the current step's goal, never the step's position in the sequence. Top center, clear of Roblox's top bar and the touch movement controls, is the conventional place for it. Briefly reveal details on objective changes, and provide a clear click/tap disclosure control. Deliberately opened details should remain readable until dismissed; cancel automatic preview timers on objective replacement or unmount. Inspect both expanded and collapsed states, including long objectives on narrow screens.

**A main window keeps one fixed size.** A window the player opens and works in, such as a shop, an inventory, a quest log, a map or a settings window, is the same size whatever it holds: switching tabs, changing the selection, filtering a list or a list with few entries never resizes or moves it, and content past its space scrolls inside it. Choose each size for the largest content the window shows at each supported screen size, within the viewport's height and clear of Roblox's top bar and touch controls, and give windows of one kind the same size. Only a small transient panel, such as a line of dialogue, a confirmation or a notice, sizes to its content, so a short message never leaves a large empty box. Check both short and long content, and reset scroll position when replacing the content with a new page.

Use anchored containers, list/grid layouts, automatic sizing, constraints, scrolling, and safe insets. Preserve minimum readable text and usable touch targets. Do not shrink a fixed desktop canvas to fit a phone. Check narrow and short landscape viewports, text overflow, scrolling to the last item, and overlap with Roblox's top bar and movement controls, such as the thumbstick and jump button.

Account for visible borders, focus/selection strokes, shadows, and rounded corners when placing controls inside clipping or scrolling containers. A control whose rectangle fits can still lose the outer half of its stroke. Reserve inner padding for the largest visual extent and the scrollbar; verify the first and last controls and both side edges.

When a transition combines UI with world effects, inspect the effect through the actual scene materials and from the approach camera. Glass can hide translucent geometry behind it; a verified remedy is to hide that surface locally during the effect and restore its previous transparency on cleanup. Own and restore any cinematic camera as well. A component preview proves appearance and cleanup, not successful cross-place travel.

Keep domain state authoritative, and domain data and component props typed. Use stable keys, and keep widgets and expensive previews/ViewportFrames alive across frequent snapshots: update text, selection, health, and enabled state in place and rebuild only when layout or content changes, since recreating a preview on every click causes blank flashes and asset reloads. Own every subscription, tween, animation, timer, model, and render callback; clean them up and cancel stale work on unmount. See the component reference for package typing and artwork adapters.

## Play test: click repeatedly and inspect the result

Require Studio's supported input and viewport capture in a qualified runner; return runner setup and playtest orchestration to the caller. Test actual player-facing controls; direct state mutation is not proof a button works. Do not take over the user's host keyboard/mouse or open obstructive host test windows.

Run the repeated-click pass at the **start of a UI testing session**, when **creating or materially changing a UI**, and during **final verification / before an authorized publication**. A Play restart or routine retest within the same session is not a new session requiring the entire pass. Reuse recorded coverage; repeat an affected check only when a change, failure, or unresolved issue justifies it.

At those checkpoints, cover each affected flow:

1. Open the screen, read it as a beginner, and perform its primary and secondary actions. Check discoverability, readability, clear outcomes, and recovery from invalid actions.
2. Click each entry point multiple times, including rapid repeated clicks. Toggle the same menu open/closed, switch between every related menu, close/reopen it, and switch while state is updating. Check for stacked backdrops, duplicate submissions, stale selections, stuck focus, flicker, and orphan previews.
3. Exercise hover/press/leave, disabled controls, keyboard shortcuts, scroll boundaries, empty/loading/error states, and click outside the modal. Verify clicks cannot pass through to the world or hidden UI. Movement must resume after closing.
4. For selection/confirmation flows, change selection repeatedly, confirm, and attempt a duplicate confirmation. Verify the visible selected state and authoritative result agree; a timeout advancing the game is not proof a click registered.
5. Inspect screenshots at supported desktop, narrow, and landscape phone/tablet sizes when UI changes. Apply the whole-screen composition criteria above; read every instruction, inspect all four button edges and rounded corners, and observe a complete pointer bounce at each affected tutorial step. Check its full motion envelope for text overlap and clipping, including while scrolling. A bounding-box assertion, successful click, or clean lint result is not visual acceptance. Record what was visually inspected; do not infer coverage from a different step or screen. Operate touch controls and scrolling in the simulator, including safe areas. Do not claim physical-device coverage from simulation.
6. Repeat the flow after character replacement, encounter exit, or another relevant lifecycle boundary. Verify camera/input restore, previews remain stable, and connections or animation work do not accumulate.
7. For UI-owned world-space text, apply `roblox-worldbuilding`'s world-space inspection to initial and changed presentations; other UI screenshots and successful gameplay do not cover it.

Fix visible or interaction defects discovered in the affected flow even when lint and builds pass. Keep evidence of actual outcomes. Scale broader gameplay coverage to the change and distinguish Studio, device simulation, persistence, and published-client verification.

## Startup and transient feedback

For loading that can make the player wonder whether the game is stuck, show a visible
progress bar and a plain-language description of the current stage. Drive determinate
progress from measured work; use an indeterminate bar while the amount of remaining
work is unknown. Never invent percentages with a timer or show completion before the
player can continue. Asset loading alone does not establish that the profile, world,
character, or destination is ready. Keep feedback responsive through each required
stage, distinguish slow progress from failure, and provide a bounded timeout with
actionable recovery instead of an endless loading screen. Verify fast, delayed,
stalled, and failed loads on desktop and touch layouts, including reduced motion and
the transition from loading to usable gameplay.

Exercise a fast first interaction as well as the settled screen. If a request starts a busy UI state before its required data or character is ready, the server must acknowledge a temporary rejection with recoverable feedback; silently returning can leave the UI stuck. Verify that the same visible control becomes usable again and succeeds once its prerequisites are ready. Keep authority checks.
