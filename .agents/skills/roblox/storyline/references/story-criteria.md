# Story quality criteria

A strong story has a clear initial desire, a disruption the player understands, escalating consequences, a midpoint that changes interpretation, a costly or revealing choice, and a resolved climax followed by a visible aftermath. The player causes the decisive outcome through the game's verbs. Character motives exist before the player arrives and change under pressure.

Characters have distinct voices, wants, needs, fears, limitations, secrets, relationships, and reasons to help or oppose one another. Opposition has understandable motives without requiring moral equivalence. Reveals have observable setup and later payoff; a twist must explain prior evidence rather than contradict it. NPCs contribute actions and consequences, not only exposition. Dialogue is concise enough to play, with deeper optional material where appropriate.

Each world has a local dramatic question, visual identity, inhabitants, playable activities, arrival/departure logic, and a satisfying local resolution. Regions connect geographically and causally. Expansion earns its size; repeated tasks change context, stakes, strategy, or relationships. Safe return visits acknowledge completed events and cannot accidentally reset or re-award progression.

The story web includes causal, relationship, knowledge, location, prerequisite, and setup/payoff edges. Dependencies have no impossible cycles; optional content is never required knowledge for a mandatory reveal. Co-op players and returning players can understand their own current objective. The journey starts at first launch: onboarding beats teach while advancing character or conflict, and crucial information survives skipped optional dialogue through readable objective/recap text.

An opening fails when the player cannot explain the immediate stakes or when its actions have no visible consequence. Review onboarding placement, purposeful geography, first-use vocabulary, and concrete character visuals alongside the later plot. Functional quest progression alone does not establish an interesting story.

## Opening, onboarding, and character appearance

Write the first few minutes as actions a new player can understand: why they are here, what changed, who or what is at risk, what they can do, and the visible result. Give geography a purpose: inhabitants, threats, props, routes, and safe spaces belong to the conflict. Teach each unfamiliar term when the player first uses it, then retain that knowledge in the canon. Respect the requested difficulty contract; if a guided sequence promises success, specify deterministic legal actions, failure prevention, and interruption/resume behavior rather than staging an unwinnable fight.

Place customization, naming, loadout setup, menus, and other onboarding steps deliberately in the emotional pacing. Explain why the player pauses there and what begins afterward. A safe respite can introduce ordinary customization without treating it as earned equipment, graduation, or a reward. Do not assume all games need customization after a tutorial; choose from the actual brief and story.

For every major character, propose silhouette, scale, face, clothing, palette, materials, props, movement, and intended emotional impression. Tie design choices to role and relationships. A frightening antagonist needs concrete readable features and behavior before and after a reversal: specify which visual and movement cues preserve menace when power, armor, or status is lost. “Scary” or “still recognizable” alone is not a design. Specify the model/rig/effects work and the player-height and gameplay-camera views that will establish whether the intended impression survives implementation.

Separate canon authority from implementation evidence. An approved written trait is established canon, not proof of a working model, scene, or animation. Keep newly invented traits, dialogue, staging, and behavior proposed even when attached to an existing character or quest. Split mixed entries into evidenced retained details and proposed additions; use unknown for unverified runtime or playtest status. Only source evidence can establish implementation, and only observed gameplay can establish playtested status.

Score each candidate from 1 (absent/contradictory) to 5 (specific and convincing) on coherence, character agency, playable variety, emotional payoff, onboarding clarity, and feasibility. A candidate with an unresolved central ending, contradictory mandatory dependencies, or a passive player climax fails regardless of total score. Scores compare design judgment; they are not measured retention predictions.
